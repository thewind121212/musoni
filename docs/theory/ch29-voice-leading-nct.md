Every example is four-part (SATB) on a grand staff: soprano and alto on the
treble, tenor and bass on the bass staff (a tenor on {C4} is marked `@b`).
Labels are Roman numerals under the chords and the chapter 11 abbreviations
under the non-chord tones (N, P, App, ET, Ant, Ret, `Sus\n4` / `\n3`); the
parallel-fifths example labels the two fifths `5`. Most explain steps have two
play buttons, the plain progression and the decorated one.

What each teaches:

1. **Non-chord tones and parallels**: non-chord tones are common in Bach's
   chorales, the model for voice leading, and are added to one voice of a
   four-part progression (our own I–IV–V–I with a neighbor and an
   anticipation in the soprano); first find the fifths and octaves between the
   voices; avoid non-chord tones that step into notes a fifth or octave apart
   (a lower neighbor making parallel fifths with the alto); two voices on the
   same beat only in parallel thirds or sixths (two anticipations a sixth
   apart). Checks: which voice has the non-chord tone; the fault an alto
   passing tone makes with the tenor; the interval for two voices on one beat.
2. **Repeated notes and steps down**: look at how a voice moves (repeat, step
   down, step up, leap of a third, larger leap); a repeated note takes an
   upper or lower neighbor (tenor); a step down takes a suspension (4–3 over
   V), an escape tone or an anticipation, and a whole step down a chromatic
   passing tone. Checks: the lower neighbor of {E5} (pad); the escape tone for
   {E5}–{D5} (pad); a suspension by sight.
3. **Steps up and leaps**: a step up takes an appoggiatura (one step past the
   goal, on the beat), a chromatic passing tone, an anticipation, rarely a
   retardation; a leap of a third takes a passing tone or an appoggiatura; a
   leap of a fourth or more is left alone for now; non-chord tones in several
   voices on one beat make consonances (3rds, 5ths, 6ths, 8ves), otherwise
   the voices are staggered (our own decorated I–vi–IV–V–I, standing in for
   the book's framework and solution). Checks: the appoggiatura for an alto
   {D4}–{E4} (pad); the appoggiatura for a soprano leap {C5}–{E5} (pad); what
   to do with two non-chord tones a second apart.
4. **Review**: a lower neighbor making parallel fifths; the chromatic passing
   tone in a tenor {A3}–{G3} (pad, {Ab}); a leap of a fourth; an anticipation
   in {G} major; an escape tone in {G} major (pad); a 4–3 suspension in {F}
   major; an appoggiatura in {G} major (pad); two passing tones in parallel
   thirds.

All examples are our own, in C, G and F major.

## Left out, and why

- **The book's figures.** 28.1's two Bach chorale excerpts (Chorales 323 and
  326) are public domain but too long for a phone step; 28.1 becomes our own
  decorated I–IV–V–I. The figures of 28.2 and 28.3 are images without alt
  text, so every example is our own, built from the text's rules.
- **The double neighbor on a step down and a step up** (28.3 figures 28.3.4
  and 28.3.10): the figures carry no text, the pattern on a step (rather than
  around one note, as in chapter 11) can't be read from the text, and in four
  parts it needs four sixteenth-note chords. Each lesson already holds seven
  steps.
- **28.4's three exercises** (add the named non-chord tones to a progression,
  realize a figured bass, voice-lead a Roman-numeral progression): they ask
  the reader to write music, which a check can't take; the review turns the
  same decisions into checks. The homework download is not linked.
- The appoggiatura on a leap of a third is taught in the text and asked in a
  check, not drawn.

## Framework gaps hit

- No independent voices: every event on the grand staff has one duration, so
  three voices can't hold while the fourth moves. The other voices are struck
  again under each non-chord tone (said once in lesson 1), as in chapter 11.
- No way to point at one chord member: labels and highlights mark whole
  events, so a voice is named in the text ("bè alto {E4}"), not marked.
- No drill trains voice leading, so no lesson has a practice link.
