Quãng level 1 trains reading interval sizes fast, which is what inversion and
intervallic change compare; nothing else in the chapter has a drill.

What each teaches:

1. **Motive**: the smallest recognizable melodic idea (Beethoven's Symphony
   No. 5 opening); developing a motive through a sequence (its restatement a
   step lower); a melody built from numbered motives (the book's four motives in
   Beethoven's Symphony No. 1 are named, not drawn). Checks: what a motive is; a
   motive sequenced up a step in C (pad); which motive a measure is.
2. **Melodic alteration**: inversion (mirror); real vs tonal inversion
   ({C} {D} {E} {G} from {G}: {Eb} vs {E}); intervallic change (rhythm kept,
   some intervals, even direction, changed); augmentation and diminution. Checks:
   a tonal inversion's last note (pad); intervallic change; diminution.
3. **More alterations**: rhythmic change (some, not all, durations); ornamentation
   with passing and neighbor tones; extension; retrograde; a sequence is not
   labelled as an alteration. Checks: rhythmic change; ornamentation; a
   retrograde's second note (pad).
4. **Fragments**: compound motive (the 7-note opening of Bach's Invention No. 1),
   two overlapping fragments a ({C} {D} {E} {F}) and b ({F} {D} {E} {C});
   Bach develops a in augmentation and b in inversion (shown with our own
   notes). Checks: fragment b's notes; fragment a inverted vs retrograde;
   fragment a in sequence from {G4} (pad).
5. **Phrase and subphrase**: the phrase as the smallest section of form, ending
   with a cadence (classical) or four measures / a lyric line (pop); usual
   lengths; the "Ode to Joy" tune's first phrase ending on a half cadence;
   subphrases a, b, primes (a', a''), a sequence stays a; motive (2-7 notes) vs
   subphrase (6+ notes, two measures). Checks: the cadence; a sequenced
   subphrase is still a; motive vs subphrase.
6. **Review**: after 11.6: name the alteration (inversion, augmentation,
   ornamentation, exercises 1 and 4), alter a motive (tonal inversion on the
   pad, exercise 2), fragment, phrase length, subphrase a' (exercise 4).

Examples: our own motives and melodies in {C} major; public-domain excerpts of a
few notes: Beethoven Symphony No. 5 (1808, first two statements of the motive,
in {C} minor), Bach Invention No. 1 BWV 772 (1723, the 7-note opening only),
Beethoven Symphony No. 9 "Ode to Joy" tune (1824, transposed to {C}: one phrase
and the second phrase's last two measures).

"Câu nhạc" (phrase) is already in the glossary.

## Left out, and why

- **Every song from the book.** 11.2, 11.4, 11.5 and 11.6 illustrate with
  copyrighted pop songs and a 1934 standard; none is named, quoted or linked.
  Their points are made with our own melodies (intervallic change, rhythmic
  change, ornamentation, retrograde, subphrases a / a' / b).
- Long classical excerpts: the development passages of Beethoven's Symphony
  No. 1 (11.1), the Pathétique sonata (11.2.4), Bach's measure-3 inversion and
  fragment developments (11.2.1, 11.3), Holst's "Uranus" diminution (11.2.3),
  Mozart's 6- and 8-measure phrases (11.4). Too long for a phone; the ideas are shown with our own notes.
- Phrase elision and periods: the book defers them to a later chapter.
- 11.5's three levels of analysis in one song: the example is copyrighted.
- 11.6's lead-sheet symbols, Roman numerals and non-chord-tone labels under
  melodies: earlier chapters' skills; the review keeps to this chapter's.
- Book order: unchanged; 11.2's seven alterations are split over lessons 2
  (the first three) and 3 (the last four).

## Framework gaps hit

- A play block is one line of events: a melody with a held bass cannot sound
  together, so the cadence example plays bass notes struck with the melody notes.
- No drill trains motives, alterations or phrase structure, so most lessons have
  no practice link.
