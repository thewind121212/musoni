What each teaches:

1. **Modulation and tonicization**: modulation as a change of key within a
   piece, the pop "key change" (a tune of ours in {C} then {D}); tonicization
   with a secondary dominant (I–V/V–V–I) against modulation, which stays and
   is confirmed by a cadence in the new key; the cadential formula
   ii⁶–I⁶₄–V–I (PD, D, D, T) in {G}, stronger than V–I alone; fast harmonic
   rhythm (Bach chorales) blurs tonicization and short modulation. Checks: the
   second key of an {F}→{G} tune (pad); what confirms a modulation; an
   {F} major phrase I–V/ii–ii–V7–I (tonicizes).
2. **Key relationships**: Baroque and Classical pieces modulate to the dominant
   (from major) or the relative major (from minor), Romantic ones further;
   closely related keys (same signature or one sharp/flat apart, five per key)
   with the book's {A} major figure as tonic triads; foreign keys. Checks: a
   key closely related to {A} major; the relative major of {D} minor (pad); the
   key not closely related to {D} minor.
3. **Diatonic pivot chords**: the pivot (common) chord and the pivot bracket,
   drawn as a stacked label (old key above, new key below); the four chords
   common to {G} and {D} major (the book's figures 22.4.2-3); the pivot's
   function (not dominant in the new key; tonic in the old, pre-dominant in the
   new: G as I = IV); the 22.2 method of working back from the cadence.
   Checks: the chord in both {G} and {D}; the pivot to avoid from {C} to {F};
   Bm as vi in {D} = ii in {A}.
4. **The new key and chromatic pivots**: recurring accidentals added to the
   key signature, flats as degree 4, sharps as the leading tone, the "sharpest
   of sharps", cancelled accidentals as tonicization or chromatic non-chord
   tones (22.5); chromatic pivots (22.6): a secondary chord in both keys (E as
   V/vi in {C} = V/ii in {G}), a borrowed chord (Fm as iv in {C} = ii in
   {Eb}), the Neapolitan bridging foreign keys ({D} minor's N⁶₅ = V⁶₅ in {Ab}
   minor, the book's Schubert relation). Checks: {F#} and {C#} in {C} → {D}
   (pad); ♭VI in {C} as IV in {Eb}; the Neapolitan links foreign keys.
5. **Modulations without pivot chords**: direct (phrase) modulation, a half
   cadence in {C} minor then {Ab} major (the book's "Kriegers Ahnung" plan);
   common-tone modulation, {A} held from V of {D} minor to I of {F} major (the
   book's Schubert trio relation); chromatic mediants and C's four (E, A, E♭,
   A♭); sequential modulation, a V7–I pair in {C} repeated in {D} and {E}
   minor, then F♯° as dominant of {G} (the book's Mozart oboe concerto plan).
   Checks: what direct modulation lacks; a chromatic mediant of C; the key of
   the repetition (pad).
6. **Review**: after exercises 22.8: a key closely related to {Db} (exercise
   1), {F} minor's relative major (pad, exercise 1), the chord in both {D} and
   {A} (exercise 7), the pivot's function in {F}→{C}, a recurring {Bb} → {F}
   (pad), tonicization or modulation, a chromatic mediant of G (exercise 9),
   and a direct modulation by ear.

Every progression is our own, keyboard style on a grand staff (bass in the
left hand, three notes in the right) or close triads on a treble staff, with a
"Nghe" button. Chord symbols are plain letters (Em, F♯m, A♭); the stacked
staff label stands in for the pivot bracket.

"Biến âm" stands for chromatic throughout, as in the glossary's "hợp âm át
biến âm". "Hợp âm chung" (common chord) appears once as a synonym of hợp âm
trụ, as the book uses both. If an earlier chapter settled another word for
non-chord tone, lesson 4 should follow it.

## Left out, and why

- **22.1's three pop songs and 22.7's film and pop examples** (Beyoncé, the
  Titanic song, Bon Jovi, Star Wars, Wagner's Rheingold excerpt): copyrighted
  or long score excerpts. A tune of our own carries the key change; the
  chromatic mediant i–♭vi progression they illustrate is covered by C's four
  chromatic mediants.
- **All Bach, Schubert, Schumann, Beethoven and Mozart score figures**: images,
  and too long for a phone. The lessons keep only the harmonic relation each
  one shows (named in the list above), voiced by us.
- **The Abschied reduction (22.2.2)** and its tonicization over several
  measures: the text says tonicization can last several measures; a
  multi-measure example would not fit a step.
- **Douglass Green's "full cadence"** aside: a citation, not needed to teach
  the formula.
- **The chromatic mediants of a minor triad** (figure 22.7.7's second half,
  exercise 9's Fm and G♯m): the text says every major or minor triad has
  four; only C major's are shown.
- **Augmented sixth pivots** (22.6.4): only named in a tip, as the book defers
  them to its next chapter (enharmonic modulation).
- **Exercises 3, 6, 8 and 10** (analysing a Beethoven song and an Elton John
  song with pivot brackets, composing a modulating phrase, motivic analysis):
  score analysis and composition do not fit checks; the Elton John song is
  copyrighted. Exercise 4 (closely related keys of the parallel minor) is
  covered by the closely-related checks.

## Framework gaps hit

- **No bracket under a group of chords**: the pivot bracket is a stacked label
  on the pivot chord (old key over new key), with key names in the text.
- **No key change inside one staff**: a modulating example keeps the first
  key's signature and prints the new key's accidentals.
- **No tie inside a chord**, so the common tone in 23.5 is held by repeating
  {A4} in each chord rather than tied.

## Source notes

All eight pages (22.1-22.8) were fetched and stripped of tags, and the lessons
compared with their text (2026-10-24). Figures are images without alt text;
the voicings are ours, from the harmonic relations the text names (for
example "N⁶₅, which acts as a V⁶₅ in A♭ minor, a tritone away from D minor").
The tip that the five closely related keys are the home key's ii, iii, IV, V
and vi is standard theory, added as a shortcut. The order follows the book,
with 22.2's method for finding the pivot moved into the pivot lesson.
