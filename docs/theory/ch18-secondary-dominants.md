No lesson has a practice link: Hợp âm (`chords`) asks diatonic triads only (by
name, or by numeral in levels 5-7), never a chromatic chord or a V/x label.
Every end screen leads with "Bài tiếp".

What each teaches:

1. **Tonicization**: V resolves to I; tonicization treats another chord as a
   tonic by approaching it with its own dominant; I–ii–V–I heard against
   I–V/V–V–I (standing in for 17.1's examples); a secondary dominant is a major
   triad or dominant seventh resolving to a chord other than I; V/V read "V of
   V", the numeral after the slash is the tonicized chord (tip: not the V/3rd
   bass slash of chapter 8); why V/V and not II (ii is minor in major, II hides
   the function); the chromatic note as the leading tone that drives to the
   next chord; diminished chords (vii°, ii° in minor) are not tonicized, heard
   as I–V/vii°–vii°. Checks: label D–F♯–A going to V; the third of V/V in F
   major (pad); which chord cannot be tonicized.
2. **In major**: V/ii, V/iii, V/V, V/vi in C major, each heard resolving; V/IV
   as a triad is I itself; the raised notes C♯ D♯ F♯ G♯ as leading tones to
   the tonicized roots; the five sevenths A7 B7 C7 D7 E7; V7/IV as the only one
   with a lowered note, B♭ acting as degree 4 of F like a key signature's last
   flat. Checks: the third of V/vi (pad); V/vi in G major; V7/IV in G major.
3. **In minor**: in C minor, III, iv, V, VI and VII can be tonicized (not i,
   ii°, vii°); V/III = VII and V/VI = III carry no chromatic note; V7/VI needs
   D♭; B♭7 is V7/III or VII7; VII can be tonicized, vii° cannot; the F chord is
   V/VII going to B♭ and IV going to G or B°. Checks: the third of V/iv (pad);
   F going to G in C minor; which minor chord cannot be tonicized.
4. **Analyzing and writing**: analysis step 1 (stack in thirds; major triad or
   major-minor seventh, or stop) and step 2 (a perfect 5th below the root is
   a diatonic root), on the book's example, F♯ major in first inversion in E
   major = V6/V; writing in four steps on V43/V in E♭ major (V is B♭, a 5th up
   is F, F–A–C–E♭, 43 puts the fifth in the bass). Checks: E major going to A
   in D major; whether Em7 is a secondary dominant; the third of V7/ii in B♭
   major (pad); V7/IV in A major.
5. **Irregular resolutions**: V/V to IV (IV standing in for V); V/V to ♭VII in
   A major (♭VII also a dominant substitute); the chain V42/IV–V7/ii–ii, where
   the B♭ falls to A as the root of A7 instead of the third of IV; V7/vi to IV
   and its two readings (a chromatic iii–IV, or V/vi to VI/vi, deceptive
   within vi). Checks: what D7 does before F; what the A is in the chain;
   F's numeral in A minor.
6. **Review**: after 17.7's exercises: approach ii with its secondary dominant
   seventh (exercise 1), analyze E7 in G major, A7 in A minor and A7 in a C
   major phrase (exercise 2), write V/V in E♭ major (pad, exercise 4), ii is
   not a secondary dominant, B♭7 in C minor, E7 to F as an irregular resolution.

All examples are our own: block chords in C, G, D, E, E♭, B♭, A and F major
and C, A minor, most on a grand staff with the bass on the bass staff.

## Terms

Not in the glossary when written; chosen here and used throughout:

| Tiếng Việt | English |
|---|---|
| hợp âm át phụ (át phụ) | secondary dominant |
| ly điệu | tonicization (tonicize) |
| biến âm / nốt biến âm | chromatic / chromatic note |
| giải quyết | resolve, resolution |
| giải quyết bất thường | irregular resolution |
| giải quyết đánh lừa | deceptive resolution |

"Ly điệu" is the standard Vietnamese harmony term for tonicization (a brief
move to another chord's key, as against "chuyển giọng", modulation).

Roman numerals follow the book: `V/V`, `V7/ii`, `V/vii°`, and figured-bass
inversions written inline on one line (`V6/V`, `V43/V`, `V42/IV`), each
glossed the first time ("6 marks first inversion", "43 puts the fifth in the
bass", "42 puts the seventh in the bass").

## Left out, and why

- **Every song from the book.** 17.1 and 17.6 illustrate with pop songs
  (McCartney, Cheap Trick, Chicago, the Supremes, Otis Redding, Lennon and
  others): copyrighted, so none is named, quoted or linked. Their progressions
  are generic and shown in our own voicings (I–V/V–IV–I, I–V/V–♭VII–I in A
  major, I–V42/IV–V7/ii–ii, I–V7/vi–IV–V–I).
- 17.1's public-domain excerpts (Verdi, Tchaikovsky) and 17.6's Beethoven
  (Waldstein sonata, Op. 53): they need score excerpts longer than a phone
  step; the Waldstein is named only, as a classical use of V7/vi to IV.
- The book's figures (all images, no alt text): the tables of secondary
  dominants in major and minor (17.3) are redrawn as our own staves in C major
  and C minor; 17.2's examples are rebuilt from the text around them
  (D7/F♯ as V65/V, E7/G♯ as V65/vi, the V/vii° example).
- Seventh-chord inversions in analysis (17.2's V65/V and V65/vi): mentioned in
  the source notes only; lesson 4 shows one first-inversion triad and one 43
  chord, which is enough to show "invert as marked" on a phone.
- 17.7 exercises 3 and 5 (non-chord tones and accompaniment textures,
  altering motifs): composition tasks with no check form; exercise 1 and 4's
  lead-sheet symbols become chord-symbol choices.

## Framework gaps hit

- None blocking. Figured bass with a slash (V⁶₅/V) cannot be stacked under a
  note as one label (a stacked label centres each line), so inversion figures
  are written inline (`V43/V`).
- No drill trains secondary dominants, so no lesson has a practice link.
