The blues lesson's link trains finding I, IV and V in a key (the Roman numeral
level), not the form itself.

What each teaches:

1. **Verse-chorus form**: form as the order of sections; verse against chorus,
   the hook in the chorus; pre-chorus and post-chorus; introduction, interlude,
   solo and bridge, with a sample song map; section names can be disputed;
   simple (one progression for verse and chorus) against contrasting
   verse-chorus form. Checks: where the hook is; what sits between verse and
   chorus; simple or contrasting (staff and sound).
2. **AABA and ABAC**: AABA from 1930s Broadway to the 1950s, 8-bar sections,
   32 bars, A the main melody, B the bridge or middle eight; instrumental A
   sections and AABAABA; the "Ode to Joy" theme as a small AABA; ABAC (32 bars,
   A returns, C new) and ABAB′; a verse before the form, which is then called
   the refrain or chorus. Checks: bars in an AABA; is the second section A or B
   (ear); a section description to name as ABAC.
3. **The 12-bar blues**: three lines of four bars, I I I I | IV IV I I |
   V IV I I; its history (early 1900s, swing, early rock and roll); the same
   numerals in G major (G, C, D). Checks: bar 5's chord; bar 9's root in F
   major (pad); the last line by ear.
4. **Closed and open sections**: closed ends on I, open on V (sometimes IV);
   in AABA the A sections closed and the bridge open; in verse-chorus form the
   sections leading into the chorus open, the chorus either; placing them is
   part of writing a song. Checks: a G major section ending on IV; which AABA
   section is open; the last root of an open verse in D major (pad).
5. **Review**: after 12.6, which asks for the sections of two songs: two
   invented song maps to name (AABA, verse-chorus), simple or contrasting,
   ABAB′, bar 5 of a D major blues (pad), which bars hold IV, and two
   closed/open sections (staff in F major, ear in C major).

## Left out, and why

- **Every song from the book.** 12.1-12.6 are built on song timelines and
  song lists (pop, rock, Broadway and jazz standards), all copyrighted, so none
  is named, quoted or linked. They are replaced by our own chord charts in C
  major (a verse, pre-chorus, chorus and bridge; an AABA and an ABAC chart) and
  by the "Ode to Joy" theme from Beethoven's Ninth Symphony (1824, public
  domain), whose four 4-bar phrases make a small AABA. The 12-bar blues
  progression itself is generic.
- The timestamps and bar counts of the song tables, and 12.6's listening
  exercises (fill in each section's start time, type and length): they need
  the recordings. The review asks the same questions of written song maps.
- The coda, which appears only in the AABA song table.
- The download link to the homework assignment (12.6).

## Framework gaps hit

- No block for a form diagram (a row of labelled section boxes): song maps are
  written in the text, and sections on a staff are separated by double bars.
- No drill trains form, so four of the five items have no practice link.
