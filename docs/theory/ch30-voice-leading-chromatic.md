What each teaches:

1. **Secondary chords**: secondary dominants follow the seventh-chord rules,
   the seventh falls by step (I–V7/IV–IV in C, B♭ to A); successive
   root-position sevenths alternate complete and incomplete voicings, and the
   raised third falls a half step to the next chord's seventh (V7/V–V7–I,
   F♯ to F); secondary vii°7/viiø7 resolve like vii°7, with the exception for
   vii°7/V to V when the seventh is above the third: write unequal fifths
   (A–E♭ to G–D) rather than double the leading tone. Checks: the seventh of
   V7/IV in G major (E, pad); F♯ of V7/V going to V7 (F, pad); how to move
   the third in vii°7/V–V.
2. **Borrowed chords**: voiced like their diatonic versions (iiø7 like ii7);
   ♭6 is a tendency tone and falls by step (I–iv–V7–I in C, A♭ to G); V–♭VI
   handled like the deceptive cadence in minor (the third of V rises, the
   other two upper voices move against the bass, ♭VI doubles its third);
   ♭III, ♭VII, i, v have no special rules, just smooth motion (I–♭VII–I).
   Checks: E♭ of iv in G major (D, pad); which note of V rises; B♭ of iiø7 in
   D major (A, pad).
3. **The Neapolitan chord**: the two rules, double the bass (the third) and
   resolve ♭2 down to the nearest note of the next chord: to the leading tone
   in V (i–N6–V–i in C minor), to the tonic in i6/4 (N6–i6/4–V–i). Checks: what
   to double in A minor (D, pad); where E♭ goes in D minor (C♯, pad); where F
   goes to i6/4 in E minor (E, pad).
4. **Augmented sixth chords**: ♯4, the salient accidental of all three, rises
   to 5 (iv6–It+6–V in C minor, with the Italian's doubled tonic); also to the
   cadential six-four (Ger+6–i6/4–V); the enharmonic German sixth in major,
   ♯2 and ♯4 both rising to I6/4 (C major); the exception, ♯4 falling to ♮4
   when the chord goes to V7 (Fr+6–V7), and smooth motion for any other goal.
   Checks: D♯ in A minor (E, pad); A♯ of EnGer+6 in G major (B, pad); A♯ of a
   French sixth going to V7 in E minor (A, pad).
5. **Review**, after 29.5's exercises (realize a figured bass or a Roman
   numeral progression in four parts): the seventh of V7/IV in F major (D,
   pad); a wrongly doubled N6 (read and heard); D♭ of iv in F major (C, pad);
   A♯ of an Italian sixth in E minor (B, pad); naming a German sixth in a
   D minor progression (read and heard); the vii°7/V exception; F♯ of an
   augmented sixth going to V7 (F, pad).

Labels too long for one line are stacked: `vii°7` over `/V`, `EnGer` over

given; "hòa âm biến âm" is also common Vietnamese for chromatic harmony, but
"hợp âm biến âm" matches the book's "chromatic harmonies" (chords).

## Left out, and why

- **Every figure.** The book's figures are images with empty alt text, so all
  progressions are our own, written from standard part-writing practice in
  keys we chose. No music was quoted; the section has no copyrighted examples.
- **29.5's exercises** are full figured-bass and Roman-numeral realizations
  with non-chord tones, which the framework cannot ask (no part-writing
  input). The review asks single resolution and doubling decisions instead.
- **The cross-reference to Principle 26.7.4** (deceptive cadence in minor) is
  stated in place, without the earlier chapter's wording.

Added from standard theory, not in the book's short text: the Italian sixth
doubling the tonic, not doubling ♭2 in N6, ♭VI doubling its third after V,
and the German sixth's parallel fifths as the reason it often goes through
the cadential six-four. Each is one sentence explaining the book's figures.
