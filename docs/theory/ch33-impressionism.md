No practice links: no drill trains modes, extended chords, planing, quartal
harmony or polychords (port guide §4). The chords drill names triads and
Roman numerals only. Every end screen leads with "Bài tiếp".

What each teaches:

1. **Modes**: Impressionism (Debussy, Ravel) and the chapter's three
   techniques; church modes as an alternative to Wagner's chromaticism. Dorian =
   natural minor with a raised 6th (Ravel's Piano Trio opens in Dorian);
   Phrygian = natural minor with a lowered 2nd (Debussy's String Quartet opens
   in G Phrygian), so the seventh chord on 5 is half-diminished (D7 against
   Dø7); Lydian = major with a raised 4th (Debussy's Danse profane; the end of
   Ravel's trio movement). Checks: 6th of A Dorian (F♯), 2nd of E Phrygian (F,
   sharps shown), 4th of C Lydian (F♯).
2. **Upper extensions**: stacking 3rds past the 7th gives the 9th, 11th, 13th
   (C13 spelled out); Debussy's E♭m9 with the 9th in the melody (Clair de lune,
   bar 15); altered ♭9, ♯9, ♯11 (Debussy's D♭7 with alternating ♯9/♭9 and a
   later ♯11 in La Puerta del Vino, shown on C7). Checks: 9th of Am9 (B), ♯9
   of C7 (D♯), ♯11 of C7 (F♯).
3. **Planing**: traditional voice leading forbids parallel 5ths and 8ves;
   Debussy and Ravel move one voicing in parallel. Dominant sevenths rising by
   half steps keep their quality (Faun); planing on the notes of the key
   changes quality (Faun, the second passage); Ravel's parallel dominant ninths
   (Pavane), and exercise 32.5.2 done: London Bridge with each melody note the
   9th of a dominant ninth (F9 G9 F9 E♭9 | D9 E♭9 F9). Checks: the next chord
   after C7 C♯7 D7; are C Dm Em the same quality; the root under a 9th of E.
4. **Pandiatonicism and quartal harmony**: pandiatonicism (all diatonic notes,
   no tonal function, a wash of the major scale, chords with a 2nd); tertian
   vs quartal, quintal and secundal stacking, new after 1900. Checks: which
   notes pandiatonicism uses; the next perfect 4th above E A D (G); C G D A is
   quintal.
5. **Polychords**: two triads at once (or sevenths, or more than two); written
   with a horizontal line, not a slash, which is a slash chord; inversions need
   not be shown. Checks: D/C is a slash chord; the upper chord of F under G; the
   root of an inverted upper chord (B♭ in second inversion).
6. **Review**: G Lydian's 4th, which mode lowers the 2nd, the ø7 on 5 in
   Phrygian (exercise 32.5.1's modal chord analysis), the 9th of G9, the root
   under a 9th of G (exercise 32.5.2), a six-note stack of perfect 4ths
   (exercise 32.5.3), V in pandiatonicism, how a polychord is written.

## Left out, and why

- **Every score excerpt from the book.** The Debussy and Ravel figures are
  public domain but long piano or ensemble scores; the lessons name the pieces
  and bars the book cites and show the same chord or technique in a short
  example of our own (D Dorian, G Phrygian, E♭m9, C7 with alterations, parallel
  C7 C♯7 D7, Cmaj7 Dm7 Em7 Fmaj7).
- **Copyrighted works, not even named:** Stravinsky (*Petrushka*, *The Rite of
  Spring* and its "Rite of Spring chord"), Copland (*Appalachian Spring*), the
  CHiPs and Jeopardy! themes, Bartók's Piano Concerto No. 2 and Schoenberg's
  Chamber Symphony. Their examples (pandiatonic wash, quartal and quintal
  chords, polychords such as A under E) are replaced by our own chords. The
  book's 32.4.3 G–F–C cycle over rising sevenths was not used.
- **Whole-tone and pentatonic scales:** book 32.1 does not cover them (only
  modes, upper extensions and planing), so they are not taught here.
- **Wagner's Tristan prelude** (a listening link in 32.1): history aside.
- **Exercise 32.5.1 in full** (London Bridge in Phrygian with lead-sheet and
  Roman numeral analysis) and **32.5.4** (London Bridge in given polychords):
  the exercise images carry no text, and the app cannot write chords; the
  review asks one modal chord and one polychord fact instead. Exercise 32.5.3's
  four six-note revoicings became one check (recognise the quartal stack).

## Framework notes

- A polychord can't be drawn split across a grand staff: the grand staff puts
  each chord event on one staff (by its lowest note), so polychords are shown
  as one six-note chord on a treble staff.
- No stacked polychord symbol (one chord over a horizontal line): the notation
  is described in text, and staff labels show only single symbols.
- Key checks whose answer is a white key (F, B, G, D, A) show the black keys
  (`blackKeys`) so the raised or lowered neighbour is a possible wrong answer.
