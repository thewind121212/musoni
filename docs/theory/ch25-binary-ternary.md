All examples are short pieces of our own in 3/4 and C major
(`music.ts`), four-measure phrases built from one opening idea, so the
lessons can compare them: a (HC), a′ modulating to G, a″ with a PAC at home,
a b phrase over a dominant pedal, a B section in A minor, a balanced binary
and a simple binary. Staves show the melody; play blocks add a bass note on
each downbeat so the cadences and keys can be heard. Form diagrams are text
(`||: a a′ :||: b a″ :||`).

What each teaches:

1. **Sectional or continuous**: binary (A B, A A′) and ternary (A B A); capital
   letters for sections, small for phrases; sectional (A ends on the tonic
   chord, shown with a HC + PAC A section) against continuous (A modulates, the
   F♯ taking it to G, or stops on a HC); naming (sectional binary …),
   two-reprise, rounded, repeat signs. Checks: what a and a′ name; a read-and-heard
   A section ending in G (continuous); an A section ending on a HC.
2. **Balanced binary**: the binary principle first (A to a new key, B back;
   in major usually the dominant, in minor the relative major), drawn as
   C → G → C on our balanced binary; then balanced binary (B ends with A's
   cadence transposed home), always continuous, in Baroque dance suites.
   Checks: A's key in a D major binary (A, pad); B's ending against A's
   (a fifth lower, ear); balanced is always continuous; B's last note in an
   F major balanced binary (F, pad).
3. **Rounded and simple binary**: the rounded diagram, our A (a, a′ to G)
   and B (b over V, a″ with a PAC); the full name two-reprise continuous
   rounded binary; the Classical era, variation themes, minuet and trio; then
   simple binary (no matching endings, no return; A A′ allowed, B develops A).
   Checks: what makes the heard piece rounded; the name of a sectional
   two-reprise rounded binary; which kind our A A′ piece is (simple).
4. **Ternary form**: A B A, statement, digression, restatement, with our
   A minor B section; sectional ternary; larger ternaries in Romantic
   character pieces; compound ternary (minuet and trio, each a rounded binary;
   the da capo aria). Checks: B's key (ear, A minor); an A B A with a HC at
   the end of A (continuous ternary); minuet–trio–minuet (compound ternary).
5. **Rounded binary or ternary**: the three criteria, in the book's order:
   proportion (our 4-measure b against the 8-measure B), the nature of the
   contrast (motives from A; a dominant pedal or V alternating with I6/4,
   drawn as V, I6/4, V7, V over G2), era (Baroque and Classical against
   Romantic; generalities to weigh); written-out repeats. Checks: 8 against 4
   measures; a new melody in a new key; a Haydn-like case; a written-out repeat.
6. **Review**: name a heard rounded binary; sectional or continuous from a
   read-and-heard A section; the key B begins in for a B♭ major binary
   (F, pad); the diagram of the book's exercise 6 answer (two-reprise
   sectional rounded binary); exercise 4's balanced-or-rounded case; exercise 5's
   sectional ternary; the key a read-and-heard A section ends in (G, pad).

## Left out, and why

- **Every book figure** (Brahms Wiegenlied, Haydn Symphony No. 94, Bach
  Invention No. 8 and French Suite No. 6, Mozart K. 284 and K. 15mm, Schubert
  D. 41, Bach English Suite No. 3, Beethoven Op. 119 No. 9, Rachmaninoff's
  Prelude Op. 3 No. 2): the scores are images with empty alt text and far
  longer than a phone staff. All are public domain; the pieces are named only
  in the lesson files' comments, and their points are made with our own
  phrases (a balanced binary, a rounded binary of the same shape as the book's
  Mozart, a ternary). The book's form diagrams (24.4, 24.6, 24.7) are SVG images
  without alt text; the lessons give the standard diagrams as text.
- **24.1** is a three-line list of the chapter's comparisons; it opens lesson 1.
- **24.6 is taught before 24.3** (with balanced binary in lesson 2), since it
  explains why a balanced binary's A section ends in a new key. The minor-key
  case (A moves to the relative major) and "usually the dominant" in major are
  standard theory added to the book's one-line definition.
- **The YouTube recordings** linked from 24.4 are not linked.
- **24.9's exercises** (Handel HWV 491, Mozart K. 331 and K. 3, the Polonaise in
  F, Gretchaninoff's "After the Ball", Brahms Op. 56a) are score analyses. The
  review keeps their task (diagram and name the form) as diagram questions,
  using the published answers' schemes (two-reprise sectional rounded binary,
  sectional ternary, and K. 3's balanced-or-rounded ambiguity), and none of the
  music. Gretchaninoff (died 1956) is not quoted or named in the lessons.
- The homework and review-sheet downloads.

## Framework gaps hit

- No repeat bar lines (`||:` `:||`) in the notation: repeats are written in
  the text diagrams, and the "with repeats" play block plays them out.
- A play block is one stream of events, so a held dominant pedal under a
  moving melody cannot be drawn or played: the pedal G2 sounds on each
  downbeat, and lesson 5 draws the pedal's harmony as block chords.
- The compound-ternary step (25.4) is text only: a minuet and trio is too long
  to draw or play usefully.
