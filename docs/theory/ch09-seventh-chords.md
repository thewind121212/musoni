No lesson has a practice link: Hợp âm (`chords`) asks triads only (levels 1-4
by name, 5-7 by numeral), and no lesson here trains triads. Every end screen
leads with "Bài tiếp".

What each teaches:

1. **What a seventh chord is**: a triad plus a note a third above the fifth;
   the four members (root, third, fifth, seventh) named by their distance from
   the root; chords built in thirds have odd-numbered members up to the 13th
   (the 15th is the root two octaves up). Checks: the seventh of G B D F (pad);
   which of three chords is a seventh chord; the member a third above the seventh.
2. **The five seventh chords**: a type is its triad plus the 7th from the root;
   all five on C (maj7, 7, m7, ø7, °7) and, as a tip, the stacked-thirds view;
   major seventh and dominant (major-minor) seventh, the name "dominant" from V;
   minor seventh; half-diminished and fully-diminished, with B♭♭ as the 7th of
   C°7. Checks: name F A C E, D F A C and B D F A.
3. **The IV/5 chord**: the IV triad over scale degree 5 (F/G in C major); it
   shares the bass and the F with V7, so it sounds like it and pop music uses
   it in place of V7; also called a sus chord (no leading tone); it resolves
   to I. Checks: the bass of IV/5 in C (pad); IV/5 in G major.
4. **Roman numerals of seventh chords**: case gives the triad, "7" a minor
   7th, "M7" a major 7th, ø7 and °7 the diminished pair; the seven diatonic
   seventh chords in C major (IM7 ii7 iii7 IVM7 V7 vi7 viiø7) and in A minor
   with the harmonic minor's G♯ for V7 and vii°7 (i7 iiø7 IIIM7 iv7 V7 VIM7
   vii°7). Checks: F♯ A C E in G major; E G♯ B D in A minor; the root of
   vii°7 in A minor (pad).
5. **Review**: D7 by type, IVM7 in D major, the seventh of Bm7 (pad), the
   raised leading tone of V7 in C minor (pad), the thirds of °7, the seventh
   chord on vii in major, IV/5 in F major. Modelled on 8.4's exercises 1 and 2
   (identify and spell seventh chords with symbols and numerals).

All examples are our own (seventh chords on C and in C, G, D, F major and
A, C minor); no music or figures from the book.

## Left out, and why

- 8.2's two song examples (pop recordings, copyrighted): replaced by our own
  F/G in C major, compared with V7 and resolved to I.
- 8.4 exercise 3 (a pop song and Mozart K. 545): the song is copyrighted; the
  Mozart excerpt is public domain but needs a long score excerpt, more than a
  phone step carries. Its point (V7 and inversions in a real piece) is covered
  by the review's numeral checks.
- Inverted seventh chords and their slash symbols (Bm7/D, "iii7/3rd" in 8.4):
  the book's 8.1-8.3 never explain seventh-chord inversions, so a check on them
  would test something the chapter did not teach.
- Alternative symbols (Δ7, M7 as a lead-sheet suffix, m7♭5, -7): the lessons
  use maj7, 7, m7, ø7 and °7 only, as suffixes (labels on the staff). Following
  chapter 7, a chord symbol is written in plain letters as the chords drill
  prints it (F/G, B♭/C), while note and key names around it are pitch tokens
  ("giọng {C} trưởng" / "{C} major"); text that names the notes themselves
  ("{F} {A} {C} over {G}") keeps the tokens.
- Seventh chords on augmented and minor-major triads: the book defers them to
  later chapters.
- Ear checks: no drill or check type asks a reader to tell the five types
  apart by ear; every step plays its chords instead.

`RomanNumeralsOfDiatonicSeventhChords.html`, 8.4 `SeventhChordsExercises.html`).
The figures are images without text (the five types with their symbols, the
stacked thirds, the numerals in major and minor), so their contents were
inferred from the prose, the image alt text and the exercise answers
(G7 = G B♮ D F in C minor, C♯°7 as vii°7) and checked against standard theory: V7 and vii°7 in minor use
the harmonic minor's raised leading tone, the other chords the natural minor.
