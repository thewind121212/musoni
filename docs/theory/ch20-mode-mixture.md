No drill trains borrowed chords: the Hợp âm drill's Roman levels ask diatonic
numerals only (level 7 is minor keys, not chords borrowed into major), so no
lesson has a practice link.

What each teaches:

1. **Borrowed chords**: mode mixture as borrowing chords from the parallel
   minor in a major passage, "borrowed chord" as its synonym; I IV beside i iv
   in C; the lowered degrees ♭3, ♭6, ♭7, with ♭6 the most common; I straight
   to i (Strauss's Zarathustra named in a tip, not quoted); the bass line
   1–♭7–6–♭6 (C, C/B♭, F/A, Fm/A♭, G, C), where IV turns into iv. Checks: ♭6 of
   G major (pad); I–iv–I with the borrowed chord picked out; an ear-only
   I–IV–iv–I.
2. **Harmonizing borrowed degrees**: ♭6 as root of ♭VI, third of iv, fifth of
   ii°, seventh of iiø7 and vii°7; ♭3 in ♭III, i, ♭VI and ♭7 in ♭VII, v, ♭III;
   the book's point that a minor-mode degree changes the diatonic chord's
   quality (ii → ii°, V → v), not a list to memorize. Checks: D F A♭ in C
   (ii°); the third of v in F (E♭, pad); which degree B♭ D F borrows.
3. **Analyzing and writing borrowed chords**: unlike a secondary chord (V/V),
   a borrowed chord is analyzed by root, quality and inversion only; the book's
   example A C E♭ G over C in G major = iiø6/5; writing ♭VI in A major as
   F♮ A C♮; a flat before a numeral lowers the root a half step from the key
   signature, not "put a flat on it". Checks: B♭ D F in D (♭VI); the root of
   ♭VI in E (C♮, pad, with sharps shown); the notes of iv in A.
4. **Deceptive cadence with ♭VI, Picardy 3rd**: V–♭VI beside V–vi; the
   ♭VI–♭VII–I "epic" ending; in minor, borrowing from major is rare (a major I
   mid-piece acts as V/iv; raised 6 and 7 are in melodic minor); the Picardy
   3rd on the last chord (Bach's French Suite No. 1 Sarabande named in a tip).
   Checks: a C phrase ending V–♭VI; the root of ♭VII in D (C♮, pad); an
   E minor phrase ending on a major I.
5. **Review**, after 19.6 exercises 1 and 2 (name a chord with symbol and
   numeral; write a chord from a numeral) plus the lessons: the most common
   borrowed degree; A♭ C E♭ in C (A♭, ♭VI); F A♭ C♭ in E♭ (ii°); D♭ F B♭
   over D♭ in F (iv6); the root of ♭III in G (pad); the notes of v in D;
   an ear-only V–♭VI in F; an A minor phrase with a Picardy 3rd.

Progressions are drawn on a grand staff (bass on the bass staff, three or
four notes in the right hand) where a bass line matters, otherwise as close
triads on a treble staff; every one has a "Nghe" button.

## Left out, and why

- **Every quoted example in 19.1** (pop and film songs, Strauss, Brahms's
  Third Symphony, Tchaikovsky's Waltz of the Flowers): the songs are
  copyrighted; the public-domain pieces would need long orchestral excerpts.
  Our own progressions carry each idea: I–i, I–iv–I, the 1–♭7–6–♭6 bass line
  with IV then iv (the book's "Creep" point). Strauss is named in one tip
  only. ♭VII7 alternating with I (the "Inchworm" example) is not shown.
- **19.4's anthem performances and film ending**: the arrangements are
  copyrighted; the lesson describes the ♭VI–♭VII–I ending in words and plays
  our own version in C.
- **The Bach Sarabande figure in 19.5**: public domain, but named in a tip
  rather than drawn; our own i–iv–V–I in D minor shows the Picardy 3rd.
- **Exercise 3 (Chopin, Nocturne Op. 9 No. 2)**: public domain, but a full
  excerpt analysis with non-chord tones does not fit a phone check.
- **Lead-sheet symbols above, figured bass below** (exercise 1 format): the
  review asks one or the other in a choice; only the iv6 and iiø6/5 figures
  appear.
- **19.2's full figures**: images with no alt text; the chords listed are the
  standard set (♭6 in ♭VI, iv, ii°, iiø7, vii°7; ♭3 in ♭III, i, ♭VI; ♭7 in
  ♭VII, v, ♭III), from standard theory.

## Framework gaps hit

None. Grand-staff chords, stacked figured-bass labels (`'iiø\n6\n5'`) and
chord-symbol labels (`'Fm/A♭'`) covered everything.
